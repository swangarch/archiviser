# Design Modern Timber Tectonics

Make timber read as an assembled architectural system, not as a surface treatment. Let structure, secondary members, enclosure, joinery, touch surfaces, and floor each have a distinct role.

## Core workflow

1. Inspect the source and lock camera, walls, openings, floors, stairs, ceiling levels, circulation, and fixed program unless change is explicitly permitted.
2. Classify the typology, span, scale, fire and acoustic expectations, daylight direction, and visible load paths.
3. Establish a primary structural rhythm before adding secondary members or finishes.
4. Design a material hierarchy and junction language.
5. Integrate furniture, shelving, stairs, lighting, and services with the structural grid.
6. Generate or edit one view at a time.
7. Validate geometry, assembly, grain direction, repetition, and photographic realism.

Read [references/prompt-framework.md](references/prompt-framework.md) when preparing a production prompt.

## Begin with structural hierarchy

Distinguish:

- primary posts, beams, arches, trusses, or wall panels;
- secondary joists, ribs, purlins, frames, and blocking;
- enclosure, lining, acoustic systems, shelving, and furniture;
- touch surfaces such as rails, treads, benches, counters, and floors.

Align members with bays, spans, openings, stairs, and room axes. Give primary elements greater depth and continuity than secondary elements. Avoid tree-like columns, gratuitous diagonals, or parametric waves unless the structural concept requires them.

## Make assembly visible but restrained

Use a small family of credible details:

- housed, lapped, scarfed, notched, dowelled, wedged, or spline joints where appropriate;
- concealed or exposed steel knife plates, shoes, bolts, pins, and base connections;
- shadow gaps and tolerance joints separating structure from lining;
- clean interfaces at glass, plaster, stone, concrete, and metal;
- layered stair edges that explain tread, riser, subframe, and adjacent joinery.

Do not decorate every junction. Place visible hardware where forces, fabrication, maintenance, or erection justify it. Keep connectors consistent across repeated bays.

## Control timber species and tone

- Choose one pale or mid-tone structural species such as Douglas fir, spruce, pine, ash, or another credible engineered-timber finish.
- Use a slightly darker or denser timber for floors, handrails, treads, counters, and other high-contact zones when useful.
- Align grain direction with each component's length and fabrication.
- Keep knots, board variation, end grain, and lamination lines realistic and restrained.
- Avoid orange varnish, uniformly repeated grain, distressed reclaimed wood, glossy lacquer, and one undifferentiated timber color across every layer.

## Design timber floors as part of the grid

When a wood floor is requested:

- choose board width and length appropriate to the room;
- align long runs with the main depth, span, or circulation axis;
- keep joints perspective-correct and coherent beneath furniture;
- use a matte hardwax-oil or similarly low-sheen finish;
- distinguish the floor subtly from structure without creating an unrelated species;
- use perimeter or threshold details that explain movement and material transitions.

Avoid herringbone or chevron unless the user requests it. Do not let floor gloss create mirror reflections.

## Balance timber with other materials

Use pale mineral plaster, stone, concrete, clear glass, textiles, and restrained dark or bronze metal to clarify rather than compete with timber. Preserve negative space. Let daylight reveal edges, grain, and depth without a yellow filter.

Integrate acoustic absorption, lighting tracks, sprinklers, ventilation, power, and guardrails into bays and junctions instead of erasing them or scattering them randomly.

## Preserve contemporary character

Avoid rustic cabins, barns, chalets, log construction, faux-historic brackets, temple imitation, generic Scandinavian styling, and decorative wood cladding that conceals structural logic. Modern timber may be warm, but it should remain precise, fabricated, and spatially disciplined.

## Validate before delivery

Check that:

- architecture and camera respect the source;
- primary and secondary structure are distinguishable;
- spans, supports, bases, and connections look credible;
- repeated joints stay consistent;
- grain and boards follow construction direction;
- stairs, shelving, guards, and circulation remain functional;
- the floor aligns with the project grid;
- timber color stays natural rather than orange;
- lighting and materials remain photorealistic and free of cartoon outlines.
